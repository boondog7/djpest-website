What is a huntsman spider

Huntsman spiders belong to the family Sparassidae. They're large, with a body length of 13 to 25 millimetres and a leg span that can reach 100 millimetres. Their distinctive feature is a flattened body held parallel to the ground, and they typically run sideways or backwards. Colour varies from tan and brown to grey and even bright green. Unlike redbacks, they have no red marking. Unlike white-tails, they have no tail-like spinnerets that are obviously visible.

Huntsmen are often mistaken for redbacks or white-tails because all three are spiders and people are wary of spiders in their homes. The key difference: huntsmen are active foragers that wander openly across walls and ceilings. Redbacks and white-tails hide in webs or in dark harbourage. You'll see a huntsman out in the open. A redback stays in its web. A white-tail stays in a crevice.

Where you see them in Perth homes

In brick veneer homes, huntsmen favour the roof void between spring and autumn, wall cavities where they hunt insects, and the sub-floor area in raised-floor homes. On exterior walls they appear at night, drawn to lights where insects gather.

In limestone footings and slab-on-ground homes, they're often found in wall cavities, the roof void, on exterior brickwork, and under external structures such as carports and shade cloths. Their presence is usually just a sighting. They don't build obvious webs like redbacks do, so there's no cobweb to spot. They don't leave droppings in concentrated spots. You'll see them when they're moving, which is often at night or on warm spring days.

Why they appear in Perth during spring

Perth's spring season, from September to November, brings warmer temperatures and longer daylight, which triggers huntsmen to actively forage and seek mates. This is when they become most visible indoors, moving along walls and ceilings in search of insect prey. The northern suburbs' mix of older brick veneer and newer homes provides plenty of wall cavities and roof voids, ideal harbourage.

Huntsmen are present year-round, but late spring sees the highest activity. They enter homes passively through gaps in weatherstripping, open doors and windows, and cracks in brickwork. Once inside, they find plenty of smaller insects to hunt and stay until food becomes scarce or the season cools.

What you can do yourself

Non-chemical approaches are effective because huntsmen depend on a food supply of smaller insects:

  • Look before you spray. If you see a huntsman, simply coax it into a container and release it outside. They're not aggressive and will avoid confrontation.
  • Remove harbourage. Declutter your home. Unused boxes, stored clothing and piles of paper are hunting grounds for smaller insects, which attract huntsmen.
  • Seal gaps. Check weatherstripping around doors and windows, and caulk cracks in external brickwork where smaller insects enter. This reduces the prey base.
  • Reduce lights at night. Turn off external lights or switch to amber bulbs that attract fewer insects. Fewer insects at the light means fewer huntsmen drawn to the light-insect relationship.
  • Monitor. Sightings in one area of the house usually mean many smaller insects are present there too. If huntsmen keep appearing in your bedroom, investigate for food sources such as lint, dead insects or small crevices.

If you're concerned about whether a product is suitable for huntsmen, the label on any product you buy is the law. The APVMA's PubCRIS database holds every registered product's label.

What not to do

Do not assume every spider is a redback or white-tail. Misidentification leads to unnecessary panic and poor decisions.

Do not use supermarket general insecticide bombs. They're applied by you, indoors, and their coverage is unpredictable. They pose a far greater re-entry risk than a targeted professional treatment, and they'll kill the huntsman's food supply, which might seem good but often leaves a gap for a worse pest to colonise.

Do not spray if you've already sprayed. If you've already used a product and a huntsman still appears days later, get it checked. The spider may simply have wandered in after treatment. Re-spraying without understanding what you're addressing wastes money and adds chemical exposure.

Frequently asked

Are huntsman spiders dangerous?

No. Huntsman spiders are venomous but not dangerous to people. They're solitary, fast, and strongly prefer to flee. A bite is extraordinarily rare and would only happen if the spider felt trapped and unable to escape.

Can a huntsman spider bite you?

Technically yes, but it's extraordinarily unlikely. Huntsmen have fangs and venom, but they use both for hunting insects. They have no reason to bite a human and will run away given any chance.

Do huntsman spiders have webs?

No. Unlike redbacks and white-tails, huntsmen don't build webs. They're active foragers that hunt insects by sight and speed. This is why you see them running across walls and ceilings rather than hanging in a web.

What's the difference between a huntsman and a redback?

Huntsmen are large, flattened, and mobile foragers. Redbacks are smaller, have a distinctive red or orange hourglass mark on the abdomen, and hide in webs. Redbacks pose a genuine health risk; huntsmen do not. Colour and body shape are the easiest way to tell them apart.

What do huntsman spiders eat?

Insects: flies, moths, cockroaches, mosquitoes and other small prey. By hunting insects inside your home, they're actually helping control pest populations. This is why removing their food supply (clutter, dead insects) is an effective non-chemical approach.

When are huntsman spiders most active?

Spring to early summer, particularly September to November in Perth. Warmer temperatures trigger active foraging and mating behaviour. You'll see them most often at night or on warm days when they're hunting.

Can I use supermarket insecticide on huntsman spiders?

You can, but it's not necessary and carries more re-entry risk than professional treatment. Supermarket products are broad-spectrum and unpredictably applied. If you want to manage huntsmen, a targeted professional approach is safer and more effective.

Do I need professional management for huntsman spiders?

Only if they're appearing frequently and you want the entry points and harbourage addressed. A single sighting is not a sign of infestation. Persistent sightings in the same area suggest an insect problem they're hunting, which is worth investigating.
