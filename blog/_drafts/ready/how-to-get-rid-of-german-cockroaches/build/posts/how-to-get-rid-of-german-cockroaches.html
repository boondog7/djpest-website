How to tell it's German cockroaches, not something bigger

This matters because the treatment is different. The German cockroach (Blattella germanica) is light tan to mid-brown, about 13 to 16mm as an adult, with two dark parallel stripes running from behind the head down the wing covers. Nymphs are smaller and darker, almost black with a tan band. They don't fly well and rarely leave the building on their own.

Compare that with the Australian cockroach, which turns up commonly in Perth homes: bigger (around 30mm), reddish-brown, with a yellow margin around the edge of the thorax, and it flies. That one usually wanders in from the garden or under the eaves and gets picked up in a general pest treatment. German cockroaches don't wander in. If you've got them, they came in on something (a delivery box, a second-hand appliance, a shared wall with an infested unit) and they've set up an established colony indoors, usually within 1.5 metres of a food and water source: under the sink, behind the fridge motor, inside the dishwasher kickplate, around the stove control panel.

The other tell is droppings. German cockroach droppings look like coarse black pepper or coffee grounds, scattered in the corners of drawers and along hinge lines. Egg cases (ootheca) are small, tan, ridged capsules the female carries almost to hatching, then drops in a hidden spot. Each one holds 30 to 40 eggs. That's the number that makes this species different from every other cockroach in your house.

Why they're harder to shift than other cockroaches

Three things stack against you. First, the reproduction rate: a female produces an egg case roughly every three to four weeks and can produce several in her life, so a handful of roaches in March can be a genuine infestation by June if nothing's done. Second, they hide in cracks as small as a couple of millimetres, which is most of a kitchen if you look closely: under laminate edging, inside the dishwasher's electrical housing, behind skirting boards. Spraying the surfaces you can see does almost nothing to a population living inside the cavities you can't.

Third, and this is the one that trips up DIY attempts specifically: German cockroaches have developed resistance to several insecticide classes in populations overseas and, increasingly, documented in Australian settings too, which is one reason products and methods matter more here than with other pests. This isn't a scare line, it's the actual reason surface spray so often looks like it's working for a week and then isn't. The population that survives a partial treatment is the resistant half of it.

None of that means DIY is hopeless. It means the method has to match the biology: bait, not spray, and patience measured in weeks, not a Saturday afternoon.

The method that actually works: gel bait and growth regulator

This is the same core approach we use on paid jobs, just at a domestic-product scale. It has two parts, and skipping either one is why most DIY attempts stall out.

  1. Find the harbourage points. At night, with the kitchen dark for ten minutes and a torch, switch the light on fast and look at the base of the stove, under the sink, and the hinge line of any cabinet nearest a heat or water source. Where you see roaches scatter, that's a placement point, not a spray target.
  2. Place gel bait at those points, not on open surfaces. A cockroach gel bait, applied at the label rate in small dots (not smears, dots), goes into the hinge gaps, along the back edge of drawers, behind the fridge, around the dishwasher's base, and near the stove's control panel. Roaches feed on it, carry it back to the harbourage, and it kills the ones that ate it and the ones that groomed them or ate their droppings. This secondary kill is why bait reaches into cracks you can't physically get a nozzle into.
  3. Add an insect growth regulator (IGR) if the product allows it. An IGR doesn't kill adults outright, it stops nymphs maturing and disrupts egg viability, which matters because gel bait alone can miss a wave of eggs already in the pipeline. Combining the two addresses today's roaches and next month's.
  4. Stop spraying insecticide near the bait. This is the single most common DIY mistake. A repellent surface spray applied near a bait station makes roaches avoid the area, including the bait, and can split one colony into several as they scatter to avoid the treated zone. If you've been spraying and baiting in the same kitchen, that's very likely why nothing's working.
  5. Remove competing food. Wipe up crumbs and grease film nightly and don't leave pet food out overnight, but you don't need to strip the pantry bare. A hungry roach finds the bait faster, not slower, than a well-fed one avoiding it.
  6. Recheck at two weeks. Fresh droppings mean the colony's still active and bait needs topping up at the same points, not new points. A drop in sightings and droppings by week two to three is the sign it's working. If nothing's changed by week four, the product, the placement, or something else in the room (that repellent spray again) is the problem.

Expect four to eight weeks for a full clear-out from a single bait cycle, longer if the infestation's been established a while. That timeline is the trade-off for a method that reaches into the 80% of the population you never see.

What doesn't work (and why it feels like it should)

  • Aerosol "knockdown" spray on open surfaces. It kills what it touches, which in a kitchen at night is a fraction of the population sheltering in cracks. It also scatters survivors, sometimes into a neighbouring unit or the room next door, and can make a single colony split into several.
  • Bug bombs / total-release foggers. These coat exposed surfaces, not the inside of wall cavities, dishwasher housings or powerpoints where German cockroaches actually live. They're also a genuine fire and inhalation hazard used indoors and are not our recommendation for this species.
  • Bleach or vinegar down every drain. Worth doing for hygiene, does nothing for a kitchen-cabinet colony that has no reason to visit your drain.
  • Diatomaceous earth spread everywhere. It can help in genuinely dry, undisturbed void spaces, but German cockroach harbourages tend to be near moisture (dishwasher, sink cabinet), where it clumps and stops working, and heavy dust in a kitchen isn't something most households want anyway.
  • One big clean and hoping. A tidy kitchen slows growth. It doesn't touch an established colony living inside the joinery. We've been called to spotless kitchens with a serious infestation, and to genuinely messy ones with none, because harbourage matters more than hygiene once a colony's established.

Stopping them coming back

  • Check second-hand appliances (especially fridges, microwaves and dishwashers) for egg cases in the seals and housings before they come inside. This is the single most common way an infestation starts in a home that's never had one.
  • Seal gaps around pipe penetrations under the sink and behind the stove, since these double as roach highways between units in strata buildings.
  • Keep bait stations topped up for a month after sightings stop, not just until you stop seeing them, because a few nymphs can still be developing in the wall cavity.
  • If you live in a unit block, ask your strata manager whether neighbouring units have had cockroach issues. German cockroaches move along shared plumbing risers and wall cavities, so a one-off treatment in your kitchen alone won't hold if the unit next door is untreated.

Frequently asked

How do you get rid of German cockroaches fast?

There's no genuinely fast fix for an established colony, and any product promising overnight results on this species should be treated with scepticism. Gel bait plus an IGR, placed at harbourage points and left undisturbed, is the quickest method that actually holds, with visible improvement inside two to three weeks and a full clear-out over four to eight.

How to rid of German cockroaches without calling a professional?

Identify harbourage points at night with a torch, place gel bait in small dots at those points (not on open surfaces), add an IGR if the product includes one, stop any spray use nearby, and recheck at two weeks. This works for smaller, contained infestations caught early.

What attracts German cockroaches to a house?

Warmth, moisture and food debris near appliances, but the actual entry point is almost always something carried in: a delivery box, a second-hand appliance, a suitcase, or movement through shared walls and plumbing in strata buildings. They don't fly or walk in from the garden the way the Australian cockroach does.

Why do sprays not work on German cockroaches?

Surface spray only reaches roaches out in the open, which is a small share of the population at any time, and it can be repellent, meaning it pushes survivors away from treated (and baited) areas rather than through them. Documented insecticide resistance in this species is a further reason certain spray actives underperform compared to bait.

How long does it take to get rid of German cockroaches?

Four to eight weeks for a full clear-out using gel bait and an IGR, depending on how established the colony was. A drop in droppings and sightings by two to three weeks is the sign it's working; if nothing's changed by four weeks, the placement or product needs reviewing.

Are German cockroaches dangerous?

They're a genuine hygiene concern: they contaminate food preparation surfaces and their droppings and shed skins are a documented trigger for asthma and allergic reactions in sensitised people, particularly children. They don't bite and aren't considered a direct disease vector risk on the scale of, say, rodents, but the allergen link is well established and is the main health reason to treat promptly.

Can German cockroaches come from next door in an apartment?

Yes, and this is common in Perth strata buildings. They travel through shared wall cavities and plumbing risers, so a single treated unit in an otherwise infested building often sees roaches return within weeks. Coordinated building-wide treatment through the strata manager is the real fix in that situation.

Is it worth calling a professional for German cockroaches?

For a first, contained sighting, DIY gel bait is worth a try. If you're seeing them in daylight, across multiple rooms, or your own spray-and-bait attempt hasn't worked, a licensed technician's program (typically $250 to $450 including a follow-up visit) resolves it faster and avoids the spray-versus-bait conflict that stalls most DIY attempts.
