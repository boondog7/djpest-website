How to get rid of ants (Australian guide, 2026).

If you've been spraying for months and there are now more ants than when you started, you're probably making a coastal brown super-colony angry. Here's how to actually get rid of ants — the science, the species ID, the non-obvious "stop spraying" rule, and when DIY's enough versus when you need a pro.

Published 1 May 2026 · Reading time: 10 minutes · By DJ Pest, licensed PMT, Perth WA

The thing nobody tells you: spraying often makes ants worse.

Australian homes — particularly anywhere in Perth's beachside suburbs — deal with a species called the coastal brown ant (Pheidole megacephala). It is not your average ant. It forms super-colonies — networks of linked nests that share queens, workers, and resources across hundreds of metres. Multiple queens. Multiple nest sites. No inter-nest aggression.

When you hit a super-colony with a repellent spray (almost everything you can buy at Bunnings — bifenthrin-based, permethrin-based), the colony detects the threat and does something called budding. It splits. What was one nest under your patio becomes seven satellite nests scattered across your property within weeks. You then spray those, and they split again. This is why your ant problem keeps getting bigger no matter how much you spray.

"If you've sprayed and there are more ants now than there were before, you've probably got a coastal brown super-colony, and the spray is the cause."

The fix isn't more chemistry. It's different chemistry — a class of insecticides called non-repellent transfer baits. The active ingredient (typically fipronil for outdoor granular baits, hydramethylnon or hydrazide for indoor gels) is undetectable to ants. They walk through it, carry it back to the colony in their crops, and feed it to nest-mates and queens. The whole network collapses, not just the workers you can see.

Step one: identify the ant species.

Coastal brown ant (Pheidole megacephala)

Small (1.5-2.5mm), light brown, fast-moving, in trails of dozens to hundreds. Beachside Perth heavy — Hillarys, Sorrento, Mullaloo, Edgewater, Connolly. Comes inside via paving cracks, lawn margins, garden bed edges. Super-colony forming. Don't spray. Use non-repellent baits along trails.

White-footed house ant (Technomyrmex difficilis)

Small (2.5-3mm), dark brown to black, with white tarsi (feet). Indoor pest — bathrooms and kitchens, attracted to moisture. Uses sweet liquid baits or hydrazide gel placed near plumbing entries.

Sugar ant (Camponotus consobrinus)

Large (5-15mm), slow-moving, two-toned (black thorax, lighter gaster), nocturnal. Mostly outdoor, sometimes wanders inside. Sweet-tooth — sweet liquid baits work well.

Argentine ant (Linepithema humile)

Small (2-3mm), uniformly light brown, super-colony forming like coastal brown but more aggressive towards other ant species. Restricted Perth distribution but expanding. Treatment same as coastal brown.

Bull ant (Myrmecia spp)

Large (10-25mm), aggressive, painful sting, isolated nests in bushland-adjacent properties. Single nest treatment with a directed liquid product. Not really a "trail" species — they're solitary hunters.

Step two: the right bait for the species.

Stop ringing pest controllers and asking "do you have anything stronger than what I bought at Bunnings?". The issue isn't strength — it's chemistry class. There are three things you want from an ant bait:

  1. Non-repellent active ingredient — fipronil, hydramethylnon, or hydrazide. Workers shouldn't be able to detect they're walking on poison.
  2. Slow-acting — a worker that drops dead 30 seconds after picking up bait can't carry it back to the colony. Modern transfer baits take hours to kill, giving the worker time to share with nest-mates.
  3. Right food matrix for the species — sweet-tooth species (sugar ant) ignore protein baits and vice versa. Coastal brown will take both depending on colony stage.

Hardware store brands that actually work for coastal brown super-colonies in Australia: Advion ant gel (hydrazide), Termidor SC (fipronil — usually sold professionally but available to consumers), Maxforce ant bait stations. Avoid anything labelled as "kill-on-contact" or "instant action" — that's repellent chemistry that splits colonies.

Step three: place the bait correctly.

Bait placement is at least as important as bait choice. Common mistakes:

  • Don't break the trail. Place gel next to active trails, not across them. Ants will walk around any disturbance.
  • Don't combine with spray. Spraying repellent product over or near non-repellent bait makes the bait detectable. Pick one approach, stick to it for at least 4 weeks.
  • Place at the entry point AND deep inside. Inside: under the dishwasher, behind the fridge, in cabinet kickboards. Outside: along paving lines, garden bed edges, lawn margins — wherever you see continuous trails.
  • Replenish weekly until activity drops. Ants will consume gel quickly when active — a 5g tube might last 4 days at peak. Don't ration the bait.

Step four: expect activity to spike for a week.

This is the bit that sends people back to the spray can. After bait placement, you'll see more ants for 3-7 days, not fewer. They're recruiting nest-mates to the food source. They're carrying it back to the colony. This is the bait working. Trail counts then drop sharply by week 2. Full super-colony collapse takes 4-6 weeks.

If you spray at the activity spike, you've reset the whole thing. You've killed the workers carrying bait back to the queens, you've contaminated the bait sites with repellent residue, and the colony splits to escape. Resist.

What doesn't work (don't bother).

  • Boiling water on the nest. For coastal brown super-colonies there is no "the nest" — there are dozens, linked. Boiling water kills the few workers and a tiny fraction of brood at one spot. Adjacent nests carry on.
  • Vinegar / lemon juice. Disrupts the pheromone trail temporarily. Doesn't affect colony health. Activity returns within hours.
  • Baby powder, chalk, cinnamon, peppermint oil. Same — temporary trail disruption, no colony effect.
  • "Kill on contact" sprays. Splits super-colonies. Documented as actively counterproductive for Pheidole species.
  • Diatomaceous earth. Works on some species (it's a desiccant — damages the exoskeleton when ants walk through), but coastal brown ants will route around it. Useful supplementary tool, not a primary solution.

When DIY is fine vs when you need a pro.

DIY is fine when:

  • You've correctly ID'd a single small colony (sugar ants, white-footed house ants, single bull ant nest).
  • You're using non-repellent bait, you've placed it correctly, and you can wait 2-4 weeks.
  • The ants are confined to one room or one external area.

Call a licensed pest controller when:

  • You suspect a coastal brown super-colony covering large parts of your property — these need coordinated, multi-station fipronil treatment to collapse properly.
  • You've baited correctly for 4 weeks and activity hasn't dropped (means re-invasion from a neighbouring property — annual professional treatment is the realistic answer).
  • Bull ants near a play area or outdoor entertaining zone — directed treatment is faster and safer than DIY.
  • You're in a commercial premises and ant activity is a food-safety risk.

Common Perth-specific questions.

Why are coastal brown ants so common in Perth?

Sandy soil (great for nest-building), mild climate (year-round breeding), and Pheidole megacephala's general invasive success. Beachside suburbs are particularly heavy because the species thrives in the lawn-paving-garden interface that Perth landscapes favour. It's basically the perfect environment for them.

Will I ever fully be rid of them?

Honest answer: probably not "forever". A good treatment + sealing entry points + good hygiene gets you 6-12 months of clear. Re-invasion from neighbouring properties is realistic in chronic-pressure beachside zones. An annual top-up treatment for $180-$220 is the maintenance approach most Perth homes settle into.

Are baits dangerous to my dog?

When used per label, no. Bait stations are tamper-resistant. Indoor gels are placed inside cabinetry where dogs can't reach, and the active ingredient quantity in a single gel placement is well below the dose toxic to a 10kg dog. We brief on placements as part of any professional job.

What about ants in my plants and lawn?

Ants in lawns are usually doing more good than harm — they aerate soil and predate other pests. Treat them only if they're a nuisance or actively coming inside. Garden bed edges along paving lines are common coastal brown nest entries — treat those with granular outdoor bait.
