Rats & mice in Perth homes

Roof rats and house mice are the two rodents we see most across Perth's northern suburbs. Here's how to tell them apart, how they get in, the signs worth watching for, and the things you can safely do to make your home far less inviting.

01 — Identification

Roof rat or house mouse?

A quick size-and-shape check usually points you in the right direction. The two below cover the vast majority of what turns up in Perth roofs and kitchens.

Roof rat

Larger, sleek climber

  • Body roughly hand-sized, with a tail that is longer than its head and body combined.
  • Slender build, large thin ears and a pointed nose.
  • An agile climber that favours roof voids, ceiling spaces, wall cavities and high points.
  • Tends to leave larger, spindle-shaped droppings, often scattered along beams and pipe runs.
House mouse

Small, curious, quick

  • Much smaller — body around the length of a thumb, with a slim tail of similar length.
  • Relatively large ears and a pointed snout on a tiny frame.
  • Nests in cluttered, sheltered spots — cupboards, behind appliances, in stored boxes and wall voids.
  • Tends to leave small, dark, rice-grain droppings, usually concentrated near food.
General information only. Appearance varies, and young rats are easily mistaken for mice. This guide is indicative and is not a substitute for professional on-site identification. For a confident result, have one of our technicians inspect in person.
02 — Where they get in

Small gaps, easy access

Rodents need surprisingly little room. A young mouse can pass through a gap not much wider than a pencil, and rats will work at a weak point until it opens up. In Perth homes the usual entry routes are around the roofline and wherever services pass through the building.

Once they're inside the roof void they move freely through ceiling spaces and down wall cavities to reach kitchens, pantries and laundries — which is why activity overhead often shows up as droppings on a bench downstairs.

  • Roof voids & ceiling spaces

    Lifted tiles, damaged ridge capping and gaps under the eaves give climbing rats a sheltered, undisturbed run.

  • Wall cavities

    Internal voids act as hidden highways between the roof and the lower rooms of the house.

  • Gaps around pipes & cables

    Where plumbing, gas and electrical lines enter the home, unsealed collars leave an easy way in.

  • Eaves, vents & weep holes

    Open or damaged vents and gaps along the eaves let rodents slip in at roof height.

  • Under doors & perished seals

    Worn weather seals on external and garage doors leave a gap at ground level for mice.

03 — Signs of activity

What to look and listen for

You'll often hear rodents before you see them. A combination of these signs usually means there's an active problem worth inspecting.

01

Droppings

Dark droppings in cupboards, the pantry, the garage or along skirting boards. Larger spindle shapes point to rats; small rice-grain shapes point to mice.

02

Gnaw marks & damage

Chew marks on food packaging, timber, stored items and cabling. Rodents gnaw constantly, so fresh, pale marks suggest recent activity.

03

Noises at night

Scratching, scurrying or gnawing in the ceiling or walls after dark — the first sign most people in the northern suburbs notice.

04

Runways & smudges

Greasy smear marks along walls and beams, worn tracks through insulation, and a persistent musky odour where rodents travel and nest.

04 — Why it matters

More than a nuisance

Rodents don't tend to leave on their own, and the longer they're established the more there is to deal with. Three reasons it's worth acting early.

Wiring & structure

Rodents gnaw to keep their teeth in check, and electrical cabling, insulation, pipework and stored belongings are common targets. Gnawed wiring in a roof void is widely recognised as an electrical-fault and fire risk.

Contamination

As they move through kitchens, pantries and storage, rodents can contaminate food, surfaces and stored goods with droppings and urine. Affected food and packaging should be discarded.

Numbers build fast

Rodents breed quickly when food and shelter are available, so a small problem can become an established one in a matter of weeks. Acting early keeps the job — and the disruption — smaller.

A measured note. The points above describe well-established, general risks associated with rodents in homes. They are not a diagnosis of your situation. If you have a specific health concern, speak to your GP or call WA Poisons Information on 13 11 26. In a medical emergency, call 000.
05 — Prevention

What you can safely do yourself

These are simple, non-chemical steps that make a home far less attractive to rodents. They work best alongside professional treatment — not instead of it.

Seal entry gaps

Close gaps around pipes, cables, vents and eaves, and fit good seals to external and garage doors. Sturdy, gnaw-resistant materials hold up best.

Remove food sources

Store dry food and pet food in sealed, hard containers, clear up crumbs and spills promptly, and don't leave pet bowls out overnight.

Manage rubbish & compost

Keep bin lids closed and bins away from the house, and site compost bins clear of walls. Tidy up fallen fruit and seed around the yard.

Trim vegetation off the roofline

Cut back tree branches and dense climbers that touch the roof or walls — they're a direct ladder into the roof void for climbing rats.

Declutter storage areas

Reduce clutter in garages, sheds and the roof space, and keep stored items off the floor. Fewer hiding spots means fewer nesting sites.

Fix leaks & standing water

Repair dripping taps and leaks and clear pooling water. Removing easy water sources makes the property a less reliable place to settle.

Prevention only — not treatment. The steps above are housekeeping and exclusion measures any homeowner can carry out themselves. 06 — Questions

Common rat & mice questions

How do I know if it's rats or mice in my roof?
Size is the quickest guide. Rats are noticeably larger with longer tails and heavier scratching overhead, while mice are small, light and quick. Dropping size is another clue — larger spindle shapes for rats, small rice-grain shapes for mice. Because young rats can look like mice, the surest answer is an on-site inspection.
Why do I hear scratching in the ceiling at night but see nothing?
Rodents are most active after dark and tend to stay hidden in roof voids and wall cavities during the day. Night-time scratching, scurrying or gnawing overhead is one of the most common first signs people report, often well before any droppings appear downstairs.
Will they leave on their own?
Generally not. Once rodents have found shelter and a food source they tend to stay and breed, and numbers can build quickly. Removing food and sealing gaps helps, but an established population usually needs professional treatment to resolve.
Can rodents really damage wiring?
Yes. Rodents gnaw constantly to manage their teeth, and cabling in roof voids and walls is a common target. Gnawed wiring is widely recognised as an electrical-fault and fire risk, which is one of the main reasons it's worth acting early.
